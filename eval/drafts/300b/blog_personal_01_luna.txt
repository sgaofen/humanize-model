We potty trained our toddler in 10 days—but only after three methods failed spectacularly.

Our daughter, Maya, turned 2 years and 9 months on May 14, and by June we were desperate. We had tried sticker charts, which she ignored; letting her run around naked, which resulted in three puddles on the rug; and a “gentle” weekend method that involved so much prompting she started hiding behind the couch whenever we mentioned the toilet.

The breakthrough came during a long weekend at home in Sacramento, June 7–16. We stopped treating potty training like a test she had to pass and started treating it like a routine she could learn.

On Day 1, we put away diapers completely during the day. We bought six pairs of inexpensive cotton underwear and let Maya choose the purple ones. We also placed a small floor potty in the bathroom and a waterproof mat underneath it. Every 45 minutes, we calmly said, “It’s potty time,” without asking whether she needed to go. If she refused, we didn’t argue. We simply tried again later.

The biggest change was giving her ownership. She carried her own underwear to the laundry basket, flushed the toilet herself, and chose one small reward from a treasure box after successful trips—usually a temporary tattoo or a plastic animal under $2. Accidents were boring and matter-of-fact: “Pee goes in the potty. Let’s clean it up.” No shame, no lectures.

Days 3 and 4 were the hardest. We had six accidents on Monday, including one at the library in East Sacramento. I nearly gave up, but our pediatrician had advised us to expect inconsistency during the first week, so we kept going.

By Day 6, Maya was staying dry for two-hour stretches. On Day 8, she announced, “I have to poop,” and made it to the potty on time. That felt like winning a marathon.

We kept diapers for naps and overnight because nighttime dryness is a separate developmental milestone. By Day 10, she was reliably using the potty during the day, even when we went to the grocery store or McKinley Park.

What finally worked wasn’t a perfect method. It was consistency, fewer words, realistic expectations, and letting Maya feel like the process belonged to her.
