I quit a return-to-office job at a fintech company in Charlotte in March 2024, and everyone assumed it was the commute. It wasn't. It was the 11 a.m. "quick sync" that ran ninety minutes because our VP couldn't make a decision without narrating his own thought process to eleven people, four of whom had nothing to do with the outcome.

The remote-work debate keeps getting fought on the wrong terrain. Executives point to badge-swipe data and say collaboration has withered. Employees point to their commute and say two hours a day back is non-negotiable. Both sides are arguing about geography when the actual disease is scheduling. Dropbox found in 2023 that its own employees spent an average of 21 hours a week in meetings. Asana's Anatomy of Work report has been saying for three straight years that people consider more than half of their meetings a waste of time. That number didn't start in 2020. Zoom didn't invent the meeting that could have been an email; it just gave it a gallery view.

I've worked in offices with great meeting discipline — a startup in Austin, maybe forty people, where the CEO enforced a rule that any meeting without a written agenda got cancelled by his assistant, no appeal. People wanted to be in that office. Nobody was clawing back their commute from that place, because the time indoors was worth something. Compare that to the Charlotte job, where the calendar was a Tetris board of recurring standups nobody remembered agreeing to, and the office itself was fine — good coffee, a nice espresso machine, natural light on the ninth floor. The building wasn't the problem. The forty-five minutes of my day spent watching someone read a slide aloud was the problem.

Return-to-office mandates are a bet that proximity alone manufactures value, and that bet keeps failing because it skips the actual variable. Salesforce, Amazon, and Dell have all pushed hard back into five-day weeks over the past two years, and the surveys inside those companies still show meeting fatigue as the top complaint, not commute resentment. You can drag someone back to a building and they will simply have their bad meetings in person instead of on a screen — same rot, worse parking.

If companies want offices to matter again, they should start by killing half their recurring meetings, not half their remote days.
