Matt Haig’s *The Midnight Library* follows Nora Seed, a 35-year-old woman in Bedford who finds herself between life and death after a suicide attempt. In the mysterious library, guided by Mrs Elm, her old school librarian, Nora explores alternate versions of her life by opening books containing the choices she might have made—becoming an Olympic swimmer, running a pub in Australia, marrying Dan, or studying glaciology.

The premise is immediately appealing, and Haig handles Nora’s depression with warmth and accessibility. I especially liked the library’s central idea: regret makes us imagine that one perfect decision would have solved everything, when every life contains its own complications. The chapters are short and readable, and the details about swimming, music, chess, and the Arctic give Nora’s possibilities a vivid, almost dreamlike quality.

However, the novel sometimes explains its message too directly. Nora’s realizations can feel more like inspirational quotations than discoveries earned through experience. The ending also disappointed me slightly. It is hopeful, and I understand why Haig wanted to leave Nora with a clear sense of possibility, but the resolution feels rushed and overly neat after the emotional darkness of the opening. Nora’s return to her original life happens so quickly that the complexity of her mental health struggles is somewhat softened into a simple lesson about choosing to live.

Still, this is a compassionate, imaginative book with an easy emotional pull. I didn’t find every philosophical point subtle, but Nora’s journey stayed with me, particularly its reminder that an ordinary life can contain more possibilities than we recognize.
