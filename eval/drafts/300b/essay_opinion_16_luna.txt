At 8:47 p.m. last Tuesday, I ordered milk, bananas, and a bag of rice from QuickCart, the grocery app that promises delivery in fifteen minutes. At 8:59, a rider in a blue helmet left the order outside my apartment on Valencia Street. The service felt miraculous—until I noticed him sprinting back to his electric bike, already checking the next notification.

In my neighborhood, the fifteen-minute promise has changed the rhythm of the streets. Since QuickCart and DashBasket opened dark grocery warehouses nearby in 2024, delivery riders idle beside every corner store, their bikes blocking the curb cuts and bus stops. They work through rain, heat, and the late-night quiet, paid per delivery and penalized, according to several riders I’ve spoken with, when an order arrives late. The speed we experience as convenience is often just pressure transferred onto someone else.

There is a human cost to treating a carton of eggs like an emergency. Riders weave through traffic on Valencia and 24th Street, racing the app’s countdown clock. I have watched them mount the sidewalk to avoid a double-parked van, or wait outside in December darkness because apartment buildings have locked lobbies. One rider told me he made $118 over a ten-hour shift last Saturday before expenses. That is less than the price of a single delivery scooter battery, let alone rent in San Francisco.

There is also a neighborhood cost. The small markets that once knew their customers now compete with companies backed by millions in venture capital and optimized for impulse purchases. Those warehouses have no café, no bulletin board, no elderly neighbor asking about your family. They are efficient, anonymous rooms designed to turn hunger, forgetfulness, and boredom into transactions.

I still use the service. Sometimes my disabled neighbor needs groceries, or I realize at 9 p.m. that I’m out of medicine and can’t leave work. But I’m trying to be honest about what “instant” means. It does not make food appear faster. It makes another person absorb the inconvenience—on a bike, in traffic, for a wage that barely covers the cost of staying in the city. Our groceries may arrive in fifteen minutes. The bill, as usual, comes later.
