I asked an AI to write my wedding toast because, three days before the ceremony, I had exactly 47 words and none of them belonged together.

The wedding was June 14 at the Conservatory Garden in Central Park. My sister, Lena, was marrying Marcus after eight years together, and I had somehow become responsible for saying something meaningful in front of 86 people, including our terrifyingly articulate grandmother. I knew the facts: Lena once drove four hours to return Marcus’s lost passport; Marcus learned to make her mother’s cardamom bread; they adopted a one-eyed cat named Pickle. But every sentence I wrote sounded like it had been printed on a greeting card.

So I opened an AI chatbot and typed: “Write a warm, funny wedding toast for my sister Lena and her husband Marcus. Mention cardamom bread, Pickle, and the fact that they make each other braver.”

It gave me a polished speech in seconds. It was good. Annoyingly good. It had a rhythm, a joke about Pickle being “the real head of the household,” and a final line about love not making life easier, but making difficult things worth doing. I copied most of it into my notes.

At the reception, people laughed in the right places. Lena cried before I did. Marcus hugged me afterward and said, “That was perfect.”

And yet, on the train home, I felt vaguely like I had shoplifted an emotion.

The toast was factually mine. The memories were mine. I had supplied every important ingredient. But the sentences—the part people heard—had been assembled by something that had never watched Lena dance barefoot in our parents’ kitchen or seen Marcus sit beside her in an emergency room at 2:17 a.m. It knew what a wedding toast was supposed to sound like. It did not know them.

Maybe that distinction matters less than I think. Human beings have always borrowed forms: poems, vows, jokes, prayers. We use templates because feelings are often too large to organize from scratch. The machine gave me a ladder; I still had to climb it.

I’m grateful for the toast. I’m also keeping the original 47 words. They were clumsy, but they were mine.
