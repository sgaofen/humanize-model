# Introduction

Chemotherapy dosing remains one of the most consequential yet poorly optimized decisions in oncology care. Standard-of-care regimens for cytotoxic agents such as 5-fluorouracil, carboplatin, and doxorubicin were largely established through population-level dose-finding trials conducted in the 1980s and 1990s, typically fixed at body surface area–normalized amounts with protocol-mandated reductions for toxicity. This one-size-fits-all paradigm ignores the substantial inter- and intra-patient pharmacokinetic variability—often exceeding 10-fold in drug clearance—that characterizes cytotoxic therapy. The clinical consequences are well documented: undertreatment compromises tumor control, while overtreatment produces grade 3–4 neutropenia, neuropathy, and treatment discontinuation. In metastatic colorectal cancer, for example, retrospective analyses of FOLFOX administration have shown that fewer than 30% of patients receive what post hoc modeling suggests was the optimally tolerated dose trajectory.

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a principled framework for this sequential decision-making problem. Dosing is naturally formulated as a Markov decision process: at each treatment cycle, the clinician observes a state (blood counts, toxicity grades, biomarkers, performance status), selects an action (dose level, timing, or agent modification), and receives a reward reflecting the trade-off between tumor response and cumulative toxicity. Early proof-of-concept work by Zhao et al. (2011) demonstrated that a fully observable RL agent trained on simulated patient data could outperform fixed protocols in a compartmental model of myelosuppression. More recently, offline RL methods—conservative Q-learning, behavior-cloned policy constraints, and doubly robust off-policy evaluation—have enabled learning from observational electronic health record data without risky online exploration in vulnerable populations. The 2022–2024TempoRL and related dose-optimization initiatives at institutions including MD Anderson and Duke have begun translating these methods toward prospective evaluation.

Yet significant barriers separate retrospective RL benchmarks from deployable trial interventions. These include partial observability of disease burden, non-stationarity of patient physiology over treatment courses, conservative safety constraints, and regulatory uncertainty under FDA guidance on adaptive trial designs and AI/ML-based software as a medical device. In this paper, we review the formulation of adaptive chemotherapy dosing as an RL problem, evaluate the methodological strengths and limitations of published approaches, and propose a trial design—incorporating off-policy confidence bounds and clinician-in-the-loop policy veto—that we argue is a necessary precondition for first-in-human evaluation.
