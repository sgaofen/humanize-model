**Grading Papers Beats Billable Hours: My First Semester as a Teacher**

Last August, I walked out of Hendricks & Cole after fourteen years as a corporate attorney and into Room 214 at Roosevelt High School, armed with a provisional teaching license and a healthy dose of terror. I turned 42 in October, and it was the first birthday in a decade I didn't spend answering emails from my phone.

People ask why I left. The honest answer: I defended a merger in 2022 that eliminated 300 jobs in a town of 8,000 people, and I couldn't stop thinking about it. My sister, who's taught fourth grade in Duluth for twenty years, said something that stuck with me: "You keep calculating what things cost. Try calculating what things are worth."

So here's what my first semester teaching eleventh-grade civics and one section of constitutional law (yes, our district actually offers it) has looked like.

I make $52,400 a year. In 2019, I made that by March. But I've also never once eaten dinner at my desk, and I quit my migraine medication in November because—knock on wood—I stopped needing it.

The learning curve humbled me. I spent twelve years at Yale Law and Big Law, and I genuinely believed classroom management would just be "leadership skills." It is not. It took me until late September to figure out that my third-period class, thirty-one sophomores, would settle down only if I let Marcus open class with his "news of the day" recap. It's now the best part of my mornings.

The hardest week was October 16th, when I stayed late for parent-teacher conferences and six parents asked, some suspiciously, why a lawyer would want *their kids*. I told them the truth: because a girl in my second-period civics class named Priya asked whether the First Amendment protects her TikTok, and it's the best question anyone's asked me in years.

Final exams ended December 20th. When I closed the gradebook, 84% of my students passed, and one kid, Diego, told me he wants to be a lawyer now. I didn't cry, but it was close.

I still have my bar card in my wallet. Some habits die hard. But when the bell rings at 7:45 tomorrow morning, I know exactly where I'd rather be.
