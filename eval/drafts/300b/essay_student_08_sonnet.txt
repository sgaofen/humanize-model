In June 2013, Edward Snowden, a 29-year-old contractor for Booz Allen Hamilton working at the National Security Agency's facility in Hawaii, gave journalists Glenn Greenwald and Laura Poitras thousands of classified documents detailing programs like PRISM and the bulk collection of American phone records under Section 215 of the Patriot Act. His disclosures, first published by The Guardian on June 6, 2013, forced a global debate over surveillance, privacy, and the limits of state secrecy. They also forced a harder question: when, if ever, is it right to break a sworn oath and the law to expose wrongdoing?

The case for Snowden rests on consequence and conscience. He revealed that the NSA was collecting metadata on millions of Americans with no individualized suspicion, a program that a federal judge, Richard Leon, called "almost Orwellian" later that year. Whistleblowing of this kind matters because internal channels often fail; Snowden has said he raised concerns with colleagues and believed formal complaints would be buried or used against him. Utilitarian ethics can defend his choice: the harm of exposure was outweighed by the public benefit of accountability, debate, and eventual reform, including the USA FREEDOM Act of 2015, which ended the bulk phone-records program.

Yet the case against him is not trivial. Snowden violated the Espionage Act of 1917, took an oath to protect classified information, and fled first to Hong Kong and then to Moscow, where he has lived since 2013, raising questions about whether his actions were purely principled or self-protective. Deontologists emphasize that rules and promises carry independent moral weight: an intelligence officer who unilaterally decides which secrets the public deserves to know sets a precedent that could endanger lives or paralyze legitimate security operations. Critics, including former NSA Director Michael Hayden, argued Snowden had legal alternatives, such as the Intelligence Community Whistleblower Protection Act, even if those channels were imperfect.

The dilemma resists a clean resolution because it pits two real goods against each other: the rule of law and institutional trust versus the public's right to know when a government oversteps its own boundaries in secret. Snowden's case suggests that whistleblowing is justified only under narrow conditions: when the wrongdoing is systemic, the harm significant, and internal remedies genuinely exhausted. Judged by that standard, his choice looks less like treason or heroism and more like a calculated gamble that the truth was worth the cost he knew he would pay.
