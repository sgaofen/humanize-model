# Baking Sourdough at 5,000 Feet: What 30 Failed Loaves Taught Me

When we moved from Chicago to Boulder last June, I thought my sourdough game would transfer seamlessly. My starter, lovingly named "Gerald" after my grandfather, had produced two years of gorgeous loaves at sea level. Then I tried my first bake at 5,430 feet and pulled out a hockey puck with the density of a brick and the flavor of regret.

Loaves one through eight: dense, gummy centers. Loaves nine through fifteen: burned bottoms, raw middles. By loaf twenty, my husband Dave was gently suggesting we just buy bread. I refused. I'd come too far.

Here's what nobody tells you about high-altitude baking: water boils at a lower temperature (202°F here instead of 212°F), air pressure is roughly 17% lower, and dough rises deceptively fast — then collapses just as quickly. My sea-level routine of a 4-hour bulk ferment was letting the dough overproof before I even noticed. The structure couldn't hold.

After loaf thirty, I finally called Flour Bakery in Denver and begged a baker named Marta for advice over the phone. She laughed, not unkindly, and gave me the adjustments that changed everything:

First, I cut my water back by about 8% — from 350g to 322g per 500g of flour. High-altitude dough needs more structure, period. Second, I dropped my bulk ferment from 4 hours to 2.5, and I stopped going by the clock entirely. Now I watch for a 50% rise instead of doubling. Third — and this felt sacrilegious — I reduced my starter from 100g to 75g to slow everything down.

For baking, I bumped my Dutch oven temp from 475°F to 500°F for the first 20 minutes with the lid on, then dropped to 450°F uncovered for 22 minutes instead of 25. That faster initial blast sets the crust before the interior over-expands and collapses.

Loaf thirty-one, baked on a snowy Saturday this past January, came out with an ear I could have hung a picture from. The crumb was open, glossy, slightly tangy. My kids, Mia (7) and Owen (4), who had been diplomatically tolerating my failures, actually cheered.

The lesson wasn't really about hydration percentages. It was about unlearning. Two years of "knowing what I was doing" had to be dismantled before I could adapt. Gerald and I are learners again, and honestly? That's not a bad place to be.

If you're baking above 4,000 feet and struggling: your starter isn't broken, and neither are you. Adjust, watch the dough instead of the clock, and hang in there past loaf thirty.
