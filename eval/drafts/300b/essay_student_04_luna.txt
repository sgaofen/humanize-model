In *Pride and Prejudice*, published in 1813, Jane Austen criticizes the unequal inheritance system that leaves women economically vulnerable. The Bennet family’s estate, Longbourn in Hertfordshire, is “entailed” through the male line, meaning that Mr. Bennet’s five daughters—Jane, Elizabeth, Mary, Kitty, and Lydia—cannot inherit it when he dies. Instead, the property will pass to their distant cousin, Mr. William Collins. This legal arrangement explains Mrs. Bennet’s constant anxiety that her daughters must marry wealthy men, since none of them can rely on family property for financial security. Austen shows how inheritance law turns marriage into an economic necessity rather than simply a personal choice. Mr. Collins’s visit makes this problem especially clear: although he is foolish and insensitive, he possesses the future legal claim to Longbourn. When Elizabeth rejects his proposal, her mother fears that Elizabeth has foolishly sacrificed the family’s security. Charlotte Lucas, who is twenty-seven and has limited financial prospects, accepts Mr. Collins because marriage offers her a stable home and social protection. Austen does not present Charlotte as romantic, but she does present her decision as understandable within an unfair economic system. The consequences of inheritance inequality become even more serious when Lydia runs away with Mr. Wickham. Without a respectable marriage, Lydia’s behavior could damage the reputation and future prospects of all her sisters. The Bennet women therefore suffer not because they lack intelligence or character, but because the law denies them control over property and makes their security depend on male relatives. Austen’s criticism is subtle rather than openly political. Through irony, uncomfortable conversations, and the contrast between sensible Elizabeth and foolish Mr. Collins, she reveals how a supposedly respectable social order rewards male inheritance while forcing women to negotiate for survival. Although Elizabeth eventually marries Mr. Darcy and gains financial security, her happy ending does not erase the injustice that made such security necessary.
