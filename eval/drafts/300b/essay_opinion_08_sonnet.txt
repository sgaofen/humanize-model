My sister-in-law's shop, Petal & Pine, sold dried flower arrangements out of a 400-square-foot storefront in Asheville until the landlord doubled the rent in March 2025. She closed the physical location and moved everything into a storage unit off Sweeten Creek Road. I figured that was the end of it. Instead her daughter Reyna, 22, took over the Instagram nobody was using, deleted it, and started a TikTok account with zero followers.

Eleven months later, Petal & Pine ships nationally and grossed $340,000 last year, most of it from a single account run by someone who graduated in 2024 with a studio art degree and, by every boomer metric available, no business experience.

I watched her do it, and none of it looked like laziness. It looked like an apprenticeship nobody assigned her.

She posted the failures — a $600 wholesale flower order that arrived moldy, a mislabeled shipment that went to Ontario instead of Ohio — because she'd learned that "process" content outperforms polished product shots by a factor of four, something she found not from a business school but from watching 200 other small shop owners fail in public and comparing notes in a Discord server of forty-some strangers who now function as her supply chain, her focus group, and her therapist. She tracks watch-time retention the way her mother used to track foot traffic. She knows within a day which color palette will move inventory because she ran the same arrangement past three audiences and read the completion rate like a P&L.

None of this is the entitled-and-idle caricature I'd absorbed from a decade of trend pieces. It's closer to what my own father did running a hardware store in the 1980s — reading the room, adjusting inventory, building relationships with the fifteen people who'd vouch for you — except the room is 900,000 followers wide and the relationships get built in comment sections at 11 p.m.

What actually happened is that the old job — clerk a register, wait for customers — doesn't exist for a lot of retail anymore, and the kids who get called lazy are the ones who noticed first and built something else instead of a resume. Reyna isn't an outlier. She's just the one I happened to be standing next to when the business came back to life.
