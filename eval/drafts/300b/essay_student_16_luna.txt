In 2020, Uber driving looked like freedom, but the COVID-19 pandemic revealed how easily that freedom could become exploitation. An Uber driver could choose when to work, reject trips, and use the app to earn money without a traditional boss. For people who had lost restaurant, retail, or office jobs, this flexibility mattered. However, drivers also had to pay for gas, car repairs, insurance, and unpaid time spent waiting for passengers. They were classified as independent contractors, which meant they generally lacked paid sick leave, unemployment insurance, and employer-sponsored health benefits.

The risks became especially serious in March and April 2020, when lockdowns sharply reduced travel. Uber reported that its ride business suffered an approximately 80 percent year-over-year decline in April, while drivers still faced the possibility of carrying the coronavirus between passengers. Uber announced $24 million in financial assistance for drivers and delivery workers worldwide, but aid was limited and difficult for many workers to obtain. The company also required masks and introduced safety procedures, shifting some responsibility for protection onto individual drivers.

The debate became especially visible in California. Assembly Bill 5, passed in 2019, made it harder for companies to classify workers as contractors. Uber and Lyft argued that drivers valued independence and launched a major campaign supporting Proposition 22. On November 3, 2020, California voters approved the measure, allowing app-based drivers to remain independent contractors while promising limited benefits such as a healthcare subsidy and compensation based on engaged driving time. Supporters, including Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi, said the law preserved flexibility; opponents argued that it allowed profitable technology companies to avoid the costs of employment.

Uber drivers in 2020 therefore experienced both freedom and exploitation. The app offered control over schedules, but not control over fares, expenses, health risks, or access to benefits. True flexibility would require drivers to keep choosing their hours while receiving stronger protections against illness, accidents, and unstable income. The central lesson of 2020 is that independence means little when workers must accept nearly all the risks while companies retain most of the power.
