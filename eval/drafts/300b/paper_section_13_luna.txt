Across 64 healthy infants tested at the University of Washington Infant Learning and Development Laboratory in Seattle between March and August 2024, eye-tracking data indicated a reliable preference for point-light walker displays. Participants were 32 female and 32 male infants, aged 5–7 months (mean age = 6.1 months, SD = 0.7). Each infant viewed 24 trials depicting either an upright point-light walker, an inverted walker, or a phase-scrambled motion control, paired with a visually matched array of stationary dots. Trials were included when infants maintained valid gaze data for at least 50% of the 2-s presentation period; the resulting dataset contained 91.8% valid samples.

Infants directed a greater proportion of their total looking time toward upright walkers (M = 61.7%, 95% CI [59.2, 64.2]) than toward the paired control stimulus, t(63) = 5.84, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.73. This preference was also evident in first fixation patterns: infants first fixated the upright walker on 68% of trials, significantly above chance, χ²(1) = 13.76, p < .001. By contrast, looking was not reliably above chance for inverted walkers (M = 51.9%), t(63) = 1.21, p = .23, or scrambled displays (M = 49.8%), t(63) = −0.18, p = .86. A linear mixed-effects model including infant age, sex, trial type, and trial number showed a significant effect of display type, F(2, 126) = 12.47, p < .001. Pairwise comparisons confirmed that upright walkers attracted more looking than both inverted walkers (mean difference = 9.8 percentage points, p = .002) and scrambled displays (mean difference = 11.9 percentage points, p < .001). Neither age nor sex significantly moderated the preference. These findings suggest that by 5–7 months, infants are sensitive to the coherent kinematic structure characteristic of human biological motion rather than merely to local dot movement or overall stimulus energy.
