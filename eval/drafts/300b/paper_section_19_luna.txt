The glass-transition behavior of PMMA/PVDF blends is anomalous because the two components differ substantially in segmental mobility: PMMA typically exhibits \(T_g\) values near 105–125 °C, whereas PVDF has a \(T_g\) near −50 °C. Despite this contrast, differential scanning calorimetry often detects a single, composition-dependent transition rather than two independent glass transitions. This behavior indicates substantial molecular-level mixing, attributed primarily to interactions between the carbonyl groups of PMMA and the hydrogen-containing segments of PVDF. Early calorimetric work by Roerdink and colleagues (1978) reported compatibility across broad composition ranges for isotactic, atactic, and syndiotactic PMMA/PVDF blends. However, the measured \(T_g\) values did not follow a universal linear interpolation. Instead, they were described by the Gordon–Taylor equation using composition- and tacticity-dependent \(k\) values of 2.38, 1.72, and 1.39 for isotactic, atactic, and syndiotactic PMMA, respectively ([Roerdink et al., 1978](https://doi.org/10.1016/0032-3861(78)90034-4)).

The apparent anomaly becomes more pronounced at high PVDF contents, where crystallization and nanoscale concentration fluctuations can broaden or shift the transition. Huang and Zhang (2004) observed evidence of weak specific interactions, including a carbonyl stretching shift in infrared spectra, but also reported increasing heterogeneity and opacity as the PVDF fraction increased. Consequently, a single DSC transition should not automatically be interpreted as complete thermodynamic homogeneity. In 2020, dielectric-relaxation measurements of 4:1 and 3:2 PMMA/PVDF blends revealed enhanced relaxation strength and conductivity, consistent with nanoscale compositional heterogeneity ([Gonçalves et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/00222348.2020.1847393)). Thus, the unusual \(T_g\) response reflects a competition among specific interchain interactions, composition-dependent free volume, PVDF crystallinity, and processing history. Reliable interpretation requires combining DSC with spectroscopy, dynamic mechanical analysis, and dielectric measurements rather than relying on \(T_g\) alone as a binary miscibility criterion.
