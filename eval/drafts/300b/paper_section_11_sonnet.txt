Heterogeneous treatment effects have moved from a robustness check to a central object of interest in labor economics, and Bayesian methods have proven especially well-suited to estimating them under the sparse, high-dimensional conditions typical of policy evaluation. Where classical approaches struggle to report credible uncertainty for effects estimated within narrow subgroups, Bayesian hierarchical models borrow strength across groups through partial pooling, shrinking noisy subgroup estimates toward more stable population-level patterns. This idea traces to Rubin's (1981) work on parallel experiments and gained empirical traction in labor economics through applications to job training programs, most notably the reanalyses of the National Supported Work Demonstration data originally studied by LaLonde (1986).

Two developments over the past decade have accelerated adoption. First, the integration of Bayesian additive regression trees (Chipman, George, and McCulloch 2010) into causal frameworks, exemplified by Hill's (2011) Bayesian Causal Forest approach, allows flexible, nonparametric estimation of conditional average treatment effects while retaining full posterior uncertainty quantification. Second, applied work using large administrative datasets—such as Angrist, Pallais, and colleagues' analyses of job-search assistance programs, and studies drawing on Danish and German social insurance records covering populations exceeding one million workers—has demonstrated that Bayesian shrinkage substantially outperforms unpooled subgroup estimates when treatment effects vary across hundreds of strata defined by age, education, and local labor market conditions.

A parallel literature has examined Bayesian approaches to instrumental variables and regression discontinuity designs, addressing weak-instrument problems common in studies of minimum wage effects and unemployment insurance extensions. Kline and Tartari's (2016) Bayesian analysis of welfare-to-work experiments and Meager's (2019) meta-analysis of microcredit trials illustrate how hierarchical priors can reconcile seemingly contradictory findings across sites.

This paper reviews these methodological threads, evaluates their comparative performance on simulated labor market data calibrated to the 2019 Current Population Survey, and proposes guidance for applied researchers choosing between BART-based, Gaussian process, and shrinkage-prior estimators.
