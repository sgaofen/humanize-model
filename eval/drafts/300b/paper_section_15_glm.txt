# Abstract

Mindfulness training has been proposed as a low-cost intervention for enhancing working memory capacity (WMC), yet individual studies report mixed effects of variable magnitude. This meta-analysis quantifies the overall effect of mindfulness-based interventions on WMC and identifies moderators of training efficacy. We searched PsycINFO, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for studies published between January 2000 and March 2024, identifying 38 eligible studies (k = 41 independent samples; N = 3,247 participants) that compared mindfulness training to active or no-intervention control conditions and assessed WMC via behavioral measures such as complex span tasks, n-back paradigms, and the Operation Span task. Random-effects models yielded a small-to-moderate overall effect (Hedges's g = 0.38, 95% CI [0.24, 0.52], p < .001), with significant heterogeneity across studies (Q = 96.4, I² = 58.6%). Moderator analyses indicated larger effects for longer interventions (≥ 8 weeks vs. < 8 weeks, β = 0.21, p = .012), instructor-led programs relative to app-based delivery (g = 0.46 vs. 0.22, p = .031), and samples with higher baseline stress, such as students during examination periods and military personnel pre-deployment. Effects did not differ significantly by age group, meditation style (focused attention vs. open monitoring), or publication year. Risk-of-bias assessment using the RoB 2 tool classified 12 studies as low risk, 21 as some concerns, and 5 as high risk; Egger's regression test suggested possible small-study effects (p = .048), and trim-and-fill analysis adjusted the corrected estimate downward to g = 0.31. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the overall effect to outliers and control-condition type. Taken together, findings suggest that structured mindfulness training, particularly when delivered over eight or more weeks by a live instructor, yields modest but reliable improvements in working memory capacity. Results should be interpreted in light of heterogeneity and probable publication bias. We discuss implications for cognitive-training research and outline recommendations for future randomized trials, including preregistration, adequate active controls, and larger samples.

*Keywords:* mindfulness, working memory, meta-analysis, cognitive training, intervention
