Baking soda paste is the most reliable fix, and it works in about 10 minutes:

1. Make a paste of 3 parts baking soda to 1 part water (roughly 3 tablespoons to 1 tablespoon for a standard 18-inch board).
2. Rub it into the board with a clean cloth or the cut side of a lemon half, working with the grain. Let it sit for 5-10 minutes.
3. Rinse with hot water and dry immediately, standing it on edge so it doesn't warp.

If the smell lingers, coarse salt works as a mechanical scrub: sprinkle a layer over the board, then scrub with half a lemon, using the salt as an abrasive. The citric acid neutralizes the sulfur compounds that make garlic smell "stick" (the same allicin breakdown products responsible for garlic breath), while the salt lifts residue out of the wood grain. Rinse and dry.

For a board that's absorbed months of garlic and onion smell, a longer soak helps: mix 1 tablespoon of baking soda into a quart of warm water and let the board sit submerged (or covered in the paste) for 15-20 minutes, then scrub with a stiff brush against the grain.

A few things to avoid:
- Don't run a wood board through the dishwasher or soak it for hours — end grain boards especially will crack or delaminate.
- Skip straight vinegar as a first resort; it can dull the wood's natural oils over repeated use. It's fine occasionally, diluted 1:1 with water, wiped and rinsed promptly.
- Once dry, rub in a food-safe mineral oil or a board cream (beeswax/mineral oil blend) — this reconditions the wood and actually makes it more smell-resistant next time, since a well-oiled surface is less porous.

If you cut garlic often, keep a separate small board just for pungent ingredients so your main board never has this problem.
