That acetone/nail polish remover smell is completely normal for a young starter — it's a sign of fermentation, not failure.

**What's happening:** After about 48–72 hours, the wild yeast and bacteria in your flour begin producing acetic acid and esters as they consume the starches. When the starter runs low on fresh food between feedings, the bacterial activity shifts and produces acetone-like compounds. It's especially common with whole grain flours (rye, whole wheat) because they ferment faster and more aggressively than all-purpose.

**How to fix it:**

1. **Feed more frequently.** For the next 3–4 days, feed your starter every 12 hours instead of every 24. Use a 1:1:1 ratio by weight (e.g., 50g starter, 50g flour, 50g water at roughly 78°F).

2. **Check your flour-to-water balance.** If your starter is too thin, acids accumulate faster. A slightly thicker consistency (equal parts by weight) slows things down.

3. **Keep the temperature steady.** If your counter is above 75°F (like near an oven or in a warm kitchen), fermentation accelerates and the starter exhausts its food supply quickly. Aim for 68–75°F. I keep mine next to the coffee maker away from the stove and it made a huge difference.

**What to expect:** Around days 4–6 the smell often gets *worse* before it gets better — it can shift to gym socks or vomit-like odors as different bacterial populations battle it out. Don't panic and don't dump it. By days 7–10, most starters settle into a pleasant yeasty, slightly tangy, apple-cider vinegar smell with reliable rising (doubling within 4–6 hours of feeding).

**When to actually worry:** Only if you see pink or orange streaks (mold) or fuzzy growth on the surface. The acetone smell alone is not a reason to discard — your starter is just hungry. Keep feeding on a consistent schedule and it will normalize.

Source: kept the same starter going since March 2021, and it smelled like a nail salon every time I neglected it for a day too long.
