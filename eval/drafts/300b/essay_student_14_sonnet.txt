On the night of November 9, 1989, East German spokesman Günter Schabowski mistakenly announced that new travel regulations would take effect "immediately," when officials had actually intended a delayed, controlled opening. Crowds streamed to checkpoints like Bornholmer Strasse, and border guards, overwhelmed and lacking orders, simply let people through. Within hours, thousands of East and West Berliners were dancing atop the Wall near the Brandenburg Gate, chipping off pieces with hammers.

The Wall's fall was not a sudden accident but the culmination of years of pressure. Mikhail Gorbachev's policies of glasnost and perestroika had loosened Moscow's grip on Eastern Europe, and in 1989 he signaled the Soviet Union would not intervene militarily to prop up satellite regimes, unlike in Hungary in 1956 or Czechoslovakia in 1968. Hungary had already dismantled its border fence with Austria that May, letting East Germans flee west through a new route. Meanwhile, mass demonstrations grew inside East Germany itself: the Monday protests in Leipzig drew over 70,000 people by October 9, and by early November crowds in East Berlin exceeded half a million. Erich Honecker, the hardline leader who had ruled East Germany since 1971, was forced to resign in mid-October, leaving his successor Egon Krenz unable to control the unraveling.

The consequences were immediate and far-reaching. East and West Germany formally reunified on October 3, 1990, less than a year later, under Chancellor Helmut Kohl. The event also accelerated the collapse of communist governments across the Eastern Bloc: Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution succeeded within weeks, and by December 1989 Nicolae Ceaușescu's regime in Romania had fallen violently. Economically, reunification proved costly, requiring trillions of deutsche marks in transfers to modernize the former East, a burden still debated by economists decades later. Politically, the fall of the Wall is widely treated as the symbolic end of the Cold War, formalized when the Soviet Union itself dissolved in December 1991. What began as a bureaucratic slip of the tongue in a press conference became one of the defining ruptures of twentieth-century history.
