The first thing Lisbon’s housing crisis taught me about digital nomads is that “living anywhere” usually means living somewhere someone else can no longer afford.

I arrived in Lisbon in October 2022, carrying a suitcase and the usual story: I worked online, paid in dollars, and wanted to experience the city slowly. My apartment in Arroios cost €1,150 a month—more than many local salaries—and it was considered a bargain because the lease was only three months. The landlord had previously rented it to tourists through Airbnb.

At the cafés around Intendente and Graça, I met other remote workers comparing neighborhoods, Wi-Fi speeds, and visa options. We spoke enthusiastically about “community,” but rarely about the people who had been pushed out of that community. A bartender told me she and her partner had moved to Setúbal after their rent increased by €300. Her commute to central Lisbon took nearly two hours each way. She was not searching for a lifestyle. She was searching for a home.

The uncomfortable truth is that digital nomadism turns geography into an arbitrage opportunity. We bring salaries earned in richer economies into places where housing markets are already under pressure. Individually, we may be polite, curious, and careful with our recycling. Collectively, we can still make a neighborhood less livable for the people who built it.

Lisbon did not teach me that foreigners are uniquely responsible for its housing crisis. Years of speculation, short-term rentals, weak tenant protections, and stagnant wages matter more than any single group of newcomers. But nomads are part of the demand, and our language often hides that fact. We call ourselves flexible when what we really have is purchasing power.

Now, when I hear someone praise the freedom of working from anywhere, I think of that bartender’s commute. “Anywhere” is not an empty backdrop. It is a place with schools, grandparents, rent-controlled apartments, and people whose lives do not come with a return flight.

The least digital-nomad thing I can imagine is asking not only, “Can I afford to live here?” but also, “What happens to this place if thousands of people like me decide that we can?”
