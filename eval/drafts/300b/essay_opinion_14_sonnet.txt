Everyone still writes about the energy transition like it's a Bay Area product launch — a Tesla in a driveway, a heat pump startup pitching at Demo Day. Meanwhile the actual retrofitting of how the United States generates and moves electrons is happening in Houston, on Allen Parkway and out along the Katy Freeway, inside the same companies that used to just pump oil.

NRG Energy is headquartered downtown. So is Quanta Services, which laid more grid infrastructure last year than most people realize exists. Exxon's low-carbon solutions division, announced in 2021, now runs out of the same campus as its upstream business, chasing carbon capture contracts along the Houston Ship Channel — the single largest concentration of CO2 point sources in the country, which is exactly why it's the best place on Earth to build CCS infrastructure. The Greater Houston Partnership counts over 400 companies in the region now working in hydrogen, wind, solar, and carbon management, many of them spinouts or pivots from firms that spent fifty years doing seismic surveys and pipeline engineering.

That's the part Silicon Valley undersells: the transition isn't a software problem. It's steel, permitting, right-of-way, welders, turbine technicians, and people who know how to move a billion cubic feet of gas safely through a metro area. Houston has 5,000 miles of pipeline already in the ground and a workforce of roughly 130,000 oil-and-gas engineers who can, with retraining, run a hydrogen hub instead of a refinery. The HyVelocity hub, one of seven regional hydrogen hubs the Department of Energy funded with $7 billion in 2023, picked Houston specifically because the electrolyzers need to sit next to the same ports and pipelines the fossil industry spent a century building.

Silicon Valley is very good at describing the future. Houston is good at building the thing that has to actually stand up under a hurricane, get permitted by the Texas Railroad Commission, and ship product by Tuesday. Solar panels don't install themselves, and a wind farm off Galveston needs the same offshore engineering expertise that used to go into deepwater rigs.

I live forty minutes from the Ship Channel. I've watched cranes that used to build platforms now build carbon capture terminals. Nobody's giving a TED talk about it. They're just doing it.
