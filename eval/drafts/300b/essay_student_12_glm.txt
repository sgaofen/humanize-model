The disaster of Napoleon's 1812 invasion of Russia was not an unavoidable fate but the product of choices—Napoleon's own above all. The invasion grew out of a political quarrel that never justified war on such a scale: Russia's Tsar Alexander I abandoned the Continental System, Napoleon's blockade of Britain, because it was strangling Russian trade in goods like timber and tallow. Rather than negotiating or accepting a compromise, Napoleon assembled the Grande Armée of roughly 600,000 men—the largest force Europe had ever seen—and crossed the Neman River on June 24, 1812.

The preventability of the disaster is visible at every stage. First, the timing was reckless. Napoleon invaded late in June, gambling that he could force a decisive battle and compel Alexander to sue for peace within a few weeks. When the Russians under Barclay de Tolly and then Mikhail Kutuzov refused to be pinned down, withdrawing eastward and burning supplies as they went, the campaign became a chase without a victory. Napoleon reached Moscow in September—winning the costly Battle of Borodino on September 7, with some 70,000 total casualties—only to find the city ablaze and Alexander refusing to negotiate. An emperor of Napoleon's intelligence should have had contingency plans for exactly this scenario; instead, he lingered in Moscow for over a month, waiting for a letter that never came, until the onset of winter forced a retreat.

Second, the logistics were knowingly inadequate. Napoleon planned to feed his army partly through magazines and partly by foraging, but the Russian scorched-earth strategy and the vast distances made this impossible. Horses died by the tens of thousands; typhus and dysentery ravaged the ranks before a single major battle was fought. Historians estimate that of the 600,000 who entered Russia, fewer than 100,000 returned. A less ambitious force with secure supply lines, or a limited campaign aimed at Lithuania and Belorussia, might have achieved a negotiated settlement without catastrophe.

Defenders of Napoleon argue that his position as hegemon of Europe required enforcing the Continental System, and that allowing Russia's defiance would have encouraged Austria and Prussia to defect. This has some weight, but it explains the invasion's motive, not its execution. Diplomatic pressure, a show of force in Poland, or even a smaller punitive campaign were available alternatives.

In short, the invasion was a preventable disaster: the product of overconfidence, poor intelligence, and Napoleon's inability to imagine that an opponent would simply refuse to play his game. Russia did not defeat him in battle so much as his own assumptions did.
