I joined Instagram in October 2013, back when you could only post square photos and my biggest concern was choosing a filter. Clarendon, mostly. Last month, on a Tuesday evening in March, I deleted the app after eleven years and roughly 14,000 posts, stories, and likes.

It wasn't a dramatic decision. No scandal, no数据 breach, no public manifesto. What finally did it was embarrassingly small: I caught myself watching a reel of a stranger renovating a camper van in Portugal while my six-year-old narrated a drawing she'd made me, and I said "uh-huh" four times without hearing a word. She stopped mid-sentence. That pause was worse than any screen-time report.

I'd tried moderation before. Gray-scale mode, app timers, the whole digital-minimalism cottage industry. The problem wasn't the minutes, exactly — though two-plus hours a day, according to my iPhone's screen-time settings, wasn't nothing. The problem was that Instagram had become the shape of my attention itself. I experienced meals, sunsets, my own garden as pre-content. I once rearranged a plate of pasta at my aunt's house in Yonkers for better light. She still brings it up.

What surprised me most was what the habit was actually made of. It wasn't craving; it was idle hands and threshold moments — waking up, waiting for coffee, standing in line at the bodega. So I replaced the slots, not the app. Kindle in my pocket instead of my phone (I've finished nine books since March, up from three all of last year). A paper notebook for the bathroom-line impulse to check something, anything. Walking, badly and slowly, toward the subway instead of scrolling on the platform.

I won't pretend everything transformed. There were three ugly days of phantom-thumb swiping at a home screen where the app used to live. I still don't know what half my acquaintances are up to, and occasionally that stings — I missed a friend's engagement announcement and had to apologize with actual words.

But here's the thing I didn't expect: boredom came back. Real, fertile boredom, the kind that makes you notice you've been meaning to fix the wobbly chair for two years. I fixed it. Then I sat in the quiet and didn't photograph it for anyone.

If you're thinking about it: you don't need a better reason than wanting your own attention back. I waited eleven years for a good enough excuse. Turns out "I just wanted to" was enough.
