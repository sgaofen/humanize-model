Advertising to children under age 12 raises serious ethical concerns because young children often cannot distinguish persuasion from information. A 2020 report from the American Academy of Pediatrics noted that children younger than 8 generally have difficulty recognizing the purpose of advertising. This makes them especially vulnerable to commercials for sugary cereal, toys, video games, and fast food. For example, a six-year-old may believe that a cartoon character promoting a colorful breakfast cereal is simply recommending a fun product, without understanding that the company paid for the message to increase sales.

The ethical problem becomes even greater when advertising uses personal data and digital platforms. In 2019, Google agreed to pay a $170 million settlement to the Federal Trade Commission and New York Attorney General after regulators alleged that YouTube collected children’s personal information without properly obtaining parental consent. Even when advertisements do not collect data, online games can make marketing feel like part of play. A child may not realize that an in-game reward, influencer video, or “unboxing” program is designed to encourage a purchase.

This does not mean that every advertisement aimed at children should be banned. Advertising can introduce children to books, educational programs, sports equipment, or healthy foods. Businesses also depend on advertising to support jobs and create competition. However, ethical limits are necessary because children have less experience, financial independence, and critical judgment than adults. Companies should not target children with manipulative techniques, hidden sponsorships, misleading health claims, or pressure to persuade their parents. They should also avoid collecting children’s data for personalized advertising.

Parents have responsibilities, but the burden should not fall entirely on families when companies employ teams of psychologists, designers, and data analysts to influence young consumers. A fair policy would prohibit targeted advertising to children under 12, require clear labels for sponsored content, restrict advertisements for unhealthy food during children’s programming, and impose strong penalties for violations. The goal is not to eliminate children’s choices but to protect their developing independence. Ethical advertising should respect childhood as a period for learning and growth, not treat children primarily as profitable customers.
