I've been going to the Met since I was nine years old, when my grandmother handed over a couple of dollars and we spent the whole afternoon in the Egyptian wing. That was the point of the place. A kid from Queens could stand in front of a Temple of Dendur and feel like the whole history of the world belonged to her too.

That feeling is getting harder to afford. In August 2022, the Met raised mandatory admission for out-of-state visitors from $25 to $30. Then in 2025, it went to $35 — among the most expensive museum tickets in the country. New York residents can still pay what they wish, in theory, but that policy hides behind a wall of fine print. The kiosks now lead with the full price. You have to know the workaround exists to use it, and you have to be willing to tell a staffer, out loud, that you'd like to pay a dollar. For a family of four from the Bronx, that's not a discount — it's an audition.

The Met's leadership defends the hikes on simple math: post-pandemic costs, insurance, climate control for two million objects. And it's true that the museum's endowment doesn't stretch as far as it once did. But let's be honest about who is being asked to close the gap. Not the trustees who mingle at gala dinners. Not the tourists, for whom $35 is a rounding error on a $600 hotel night. The burden falls on the busload of teenagers from Newark, the nanny on her day off, the art student who used to come sketch on Tuesdays.

Cities are measured by what their poorest residents can access. Central Park is free. The Staten Island Ferry is free. The Met was supposed to be in that tradition — a public institution occupying public land under a lease with the city, partly supported by public money. A $35 gate says something different: culture is a product, and you are a customer.

There's a fix, and it's not complicated. London's British Museum and the Tate have kept admission free through economic cycles far worse than this one, because Britain decided access was non-negotiable. The Met could pursue that — real endowment campaigns, real city support — instead of quietly pricing out the people whose museum this is supposed to be.

Otherwise, someday soon, a nine-year-old from Queens will stand outside those steps on Fifth Avenue, and her grandmother will look at the ticket price and keep walking. The Met will be poorer for it. So will the city.
