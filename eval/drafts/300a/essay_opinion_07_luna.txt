Every city should copy Bogotá’s Ciclovía Sundays—not as a novelty, but as a weekly act of civic imagination.

On Sundays and public holidays, Bogotá closes roughly 140 kilometers of streets to cars from 7 a.m. to 2 p.m. Cyclists, runners, skaters, families with strollers, and elderly pedestrians take over avenues that usually belong to traffic. The tradition began on December 15, 1974, when a small citizen-led demonstration shut down several central roads. Fifty years later, more than a million people may use the network in a typical week.

The genius of Ciclovía is that it asks surprisingly little. It does not require a subway extension, a new stadium, or a futuristic fleet of electric buses. It requires barricades, traffic officers, public toilets, first-aid stations, and the political courage to say that, for seven hours, movement and pleasure matter more than parking.

I remember the first time I saw footage of Bogotá’s Carrera Séptima transformed on a Sunday. The avenue looked less like a transportation corridor than a common room: children wobbling on bicycles, teenagers practicing tricks, grandparents walking arm in arm, vendors selling juice and arepas. The city had not built a new park. It had simply returned the existing one—the street—to its residents.

That distinction matters. Most urban policies are designed around commuting: how quickly workers can get downtown, how many cars can pass an intersection, how efficiently goods can move. Ciclovía makes room for another purpose: being in a city without needing to buy anything or go anywhere.

Critics will object that closing streets is disruptive. Of course it is. Cities are supposed to negotiate competing needs, not pretend that cars are the only legitimate users of public space. A Sunday closure is also a low-risk experiment. It lets residents experience what a quieter, safer street feels like before anyone debates permanent redesign.

Every city could start small: one avenue, four hours, one Sunday a month. Then watch what happens. People will come—not because they have been instructed to exercise, but because they have been invited to belong.
