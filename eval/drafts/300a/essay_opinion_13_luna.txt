When my daughter Maya turned 9 in March, I gave her a flip phone instead of a smartphone.

It is a purple Nokia with a scratched plastic case, a tiny screen, and a battery that seems to last forever. She uses it to call me when soccer practice ends early at Lincoln Elementary, to text her grandmother in Sacramento, and to check in when she walks the three blocks from our apartment to the library on Saturdays. That is about it.

The decision was not a rejection of technology. Technology is already woven into Maya’s life. She uses a school Chromebook, watches science videos, and knows how to ask Alexa for the weather. What I wanted to postpone was the particular kind of technology represented by a smartphone: an always-available stream of notifications, videos, games, advertising, social comparison, and adult attention.

At 9, Maya is still learning how to be bored. On the bus to her swimming lessons, she looks out the window. She notices the mural on 14th Street, the man who sells oranges near the station, and whether the moon is visible between the buildings. Last week, she spent twenty minutes trying to teach herself a card trick because there was nothing else to do. I don’t want to fill every empty space before she discovers what might grow there.

The flip phone also makes communication feel intentional. If Maya calls, she has something to say. If I call, she answers with “Hi, Dad,” rather than glancing at a screen while continuing a game. We have a family rule that phones stay in backpacks during meals, and her phone is usually powered off by 7:30 p.m. There is no group chat to moderate, no social-media account to monitor, and no need to explain why a classmate’s vacation video has 400 likes.

Of course, the flip phone is inconvenient. She cannot send photos easily, use maps, or join the messaging app her classmates prefer. Sometimes she complains that she is the only kid in fourth grade without an iPhone. I tell her that “not yet” is a complete answer.

She will eventually have a smartphone. For now, I want her first phone to be a tool for independence—not a portable version of the entire internet.
