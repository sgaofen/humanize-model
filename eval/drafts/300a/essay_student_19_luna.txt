Kant argues that lying is always morally wrong because it treats other people as tools rather than as rational individuals who deserve the truth. In his famous example, if a murderer asks whether an innocent person is hiding in your house, Kant still believes you should not lie, because morality depends on universal rules, not on predicting consequences. A lie can produce unexpected harm and weaken trust in everyone’s promises. However, a lifeguard’s dilemma suggests that protecting someone may sometimes justify deception. Imagine that on July 14, 2026, at Santa Monica State Beach, lifeguard Elena Ramirez sees a panicked child named Marcus struggling in a strong rip current. Marcus’s younger sister, Sofia, is on the shore, and she begins running into the water to save him. Elena knows Sofia cannot swim well. If Elena tells Sofia, “Marcus is safe; stay where you are,” even though he is still in danger, she may be lying in order to prevent a second drowning. She can then rescue Marcus herself and keep Sofia from making the situation worse.

Kant would object that Elena is using Sofia’s ignorance to control her actions. Yet many people would consider Elena’s deception morally justified because it prevents immediate and serious harm. The lie is not selfish, manipulative, or intended to gain an advantage; it is a temporary emergency measure used to protect a vulnerable person. Still, the situation does not prove that every protective lie is acceptable. A person might claim to lie “for someone’s own good” when the real motive is embarrassment, convenience, or control. Therefore, a lie should be justified only when the danger is urgent, the harm is substantial, no truthful alternative is available, and the person lying accepts responsibility for the consequences. Kant’s strict rule protects trust, but the lifeguard’s dilemma shows that morality must also consider human vulnerability. In extreme cases, saving a life can be more important than obeying an absolute ban on deception.
