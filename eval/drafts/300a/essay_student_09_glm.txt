In October 1973, the Arab members of the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries, angered by American support for Israel during the Yom Kippur War, cut oil production and imposed an embargo on the United States, the Netherlands, and other allies. Within months, the price of a barrel of oil had quadrupled, rising from roughly $3 to nearly $12 by early 1974. The shock exposed, in the starkest possible terms, how deeply the Western economies had come to depend on a resource they did not control.

The United States, though a major oil producer itself, had seen domestic production peak in 1970, and by 1973 it imported about a third of its oil. Western Europe and Japan were even more exposed, importing the overwhelming majority of their petroleum from the Middle East and North Africa. When the embargo hit, the consequences were immediate and visible: gasoline rationing in the United States, odd-even license plate schemes determining which days drivers could refuel, long lines at pumps, and a 55-mile-per-hour national speed limit imposed in early 1974 to conserve fuel. In Britain, the combination of the oil shock and a coal miners' strike forced the government to institute a three-day workweek for industry. Japan, which imported over 75 percent of its energy, experienced panic buying of consumer goods and inflation, an episode remembered as the "toilet paper panic" of 1973.

The economic damage was severe and lasting. Inflation in the United States climbed above 12 percent by 1974, stock markets fell sharply, and the era of cheap energy that had underwritten postwar prosperity came to an end. Economists began speaking of "stagflation"—the previously rare combination of stagnant growth and rising prices—which conventional policy tools seemed powerless to cure.

Yet the embargo also forced a reckoning. Western governments established the International Energy Agency in 1974 to coordinate emergency reserves and reduce vulnerability. France launched a massive nuclear power program, and Japan invested heavily in energy efficiency, becoming a world leader in fuel-efficient technology. Denmark began its turn toward wind power. The crisis, in other words, revealed not only dependence but the possibility of adaptation.

The 1973 embargo remains a defining lesson in economic interdependence: a cartel of producing nations, wielding a resource as political leverage, could destabilize the world's wealthiest economies almost overnight. It demonstrated that energy security is national security, a lesson that continues to shape foreign policy, from the strategic petroleum reserve to today's debates over renewable energy and supply chains. The lines at the gas pumps of 1973 were more than an inconvenience; they were a warning the West has never entirely stopped heeding.
