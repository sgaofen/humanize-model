Co-parenting across time zones: managing school schedules from Berlin and Toronto

When Marcus took the teaching position at Humboldt University in September 2023, we knew our co-parenting arrangement was about to get complicated. He moved to Berlin; I stayed in Toronto with our two kids, Elena (11) and Theo (8). Six hours of time difference doesn't sound like much until you're trying to coordinate a parent-teacher night.

Here's what we've figured out after eighteen months of this.

The shared calendar is sacred. Everything goes into one Google Calendar: school events, swim practice at Wallace Emerson Community Centre, Theo's Wednesday speech therapy at 4 p.m. Toronto time. If it's not in the calendar, it doesn't exist. Marcus checks it with his morning coffee at 7 a.m. Berlin time, which is 1 a.m. for me — so he catches anything from my evening before I've even woken up.

We flip who attends what. Marcus can't physically sit in the gymnasium at Earl Grey Public School, but he joins parent-teacher interviews by video, usually at 8 or 9 p.m. his time. Elena's teachers have been wonderful about scheduling early slots. For things that truly require a body in the room — the winter concert, March break pickup — he books flights. He's been back four times, staying in a rental near us on Dovercourt Road.

The kids have "Berlin dinner" every Tuesday and Friday. That's 2:30 p.m. for Marcus, so he eats lunch while they eat dinner, and they talk about their week. Theo likes to show him LEGO builds over the camera; Elena mostly rolls her eyes but never misses a session. We protect that time fiercely — no scheduling playdates over it.

The hard parts are real. When Theo had the flu last February and I was up all night, Marcus felt useless 6,600 kilometres away. We've learned he can still help: he handles all the online stuff — ordering school supplies, booking Theo's therapy appointments, researching March break camps — from abroad. Practical support is support.

Time zone math has become the kids' weird skill. Theo can convert Toronto to Berlin in his head faster than I can. When daylight saving hits at different times — Europe switches two weeks before North America in spring — we live through a fortnight of five-hour difference and everyone gets confused.

Our arrangement isn't what we pictured when we separated in 2021. But Elena told her school counselor recently that she has "a dad in two countries," and she said it like it was a superpower. I'll take that.

If you're doing something similar: shared calendar, protected call times, and split the invisible labour by continent. It's not perfect, but it's ours.
