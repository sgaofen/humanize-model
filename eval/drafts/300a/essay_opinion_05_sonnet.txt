My father bought the Corolla in June of 1998, four months before I was born, from a Toyota dealer in Fresno that isn't there anymore. It has 214,000 miles on it. The stereo still takes cassettes. There is a JCPenney receipt from 2003 wedged in the map pocket that no one has ever removed, out of what I can only describe as superstition.

I pay $94.97 a month for things that live nowhere. Spotify, iCloud, ChatGPT Plus, a meditation app I opened four times, a New York Times subscription I get for the crossword, Adobe, a VPN, and Amazon Prime, which I no longer think of as a subscription so much as a tax. None of it depreciates the way a car does, and none of it will still exist, in any form I'd recognize, in 2054, when the Corolla will be old enough to buy its own subscriptions.

The car needs a specific kind of care: my father changes the oil every 5,000 miles himself, in the driveway, with a bucket that's older than I am. When something breaks, he can name the part. A timing belt. A knock sensor. He has opinions about NGK versus Denso spark plugs. The relationship is total but it is also, crucially, over — the day he handed over the keys, the transaction was complete. He owns a thing that owes him nothing further.

What I own owes me constant upkeep in a different currency. Terms of service change. Prices creep from $9.99 to $12.99 with an email I don't read. A feature I liked gets deprecated in favor of one that requires a higher tier. I am not maintaining anything; I am being maintained on, quietly, by fourteen different companies that each believe they have first claim on my attention and my card. The Corolla asks for oil every few thousand miles. My subscriptions ask for something smaller and more frequent — a glance, a login, a renewal I don't notice until my bank statement flags it.

I used to think the difference was ownership versus access. Now I think it's closer to this: my father's car gets worse in ways he can predict and fix, while my software gets "better" in ways I never asked for and can't opt out of. He's driving the same object he drove in 2004. I'm not using the same Spotify I used in 2019 — the interface, the algorithm, the price, all quietly replaced underneath the same four letters.

He says he'll drive it until it dies. I don't think I'll ever be able to say that about anything I pay for monthly.
