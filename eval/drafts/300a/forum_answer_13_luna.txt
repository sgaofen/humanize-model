Assuming you have a three- to six-month emergency fund and no credit-card debt, I’d first capture every available employer 401(k) match. After that, the answer depends on whether your student loan might be forgiven.

If you work for a qualifying nonprofit or government employer, verify PSLF eligibility before making extra payments. For example, someone with $42,000 in federal Direct Loans who expects to reach 120 qualifying payments should generally make the required income-driven payments and invest the extra money instead of voluntarily paying off a balance that may be forgiven. StudentAid.gov says qualifying payments and employment requirements matter, so don’t assume forgiveness without checking your account and employer.

If forgiveness is not realistic, I would probably split the difference: contribute enough to the Roth IRA to keep the habit—perhaps $3,750 in 2026—and put the rest toward the 6.8% loan. Paying down the loan produces a guaranteed 6.8% return, while a Roth IRA’s investments may earn more over decades but can also lose money. At 6.8%, this is not “cheap” debt.

There is also an opportunity-cost issue. The 2026 IRA limit is $7,500 for people under 50, and once the 2026 contribution window closes, that space generally cannot be recovered. You could pay extra on a loan next year, but you cannot go back and create unused 2026 Roth space. That argues for at least funding the Roth during 2026, provided you are eligible based on income.

For a borrower with $30,000 remaining, secure employment, no forgiveness path, and 30 years until retirement, I’d lean toward funding the Roth while making all required loan payments. If being debt-free is a major priority or your emergency savings are thin, prioritize the loan after taking the employer match.
