In September 2022, I watched my mother recalculate our grocery list three times at the Kroger on Elm Street before she put anything in the cart. That was the month the Bureau of Labor Statistics confirmed what our family had felt for weeks: inflation had hit roughly 9.1% year-over-year, the highest in forty years. For a household of five in a mid-sized Ohio suburb, that number stopped being an abstraction on the evening news and became a line-by-line negotiation over what we could still afford to eat.

Before 2022, my mother's weekly grocery run cost about $180 and filled two carts. By that October, the same list cost close to $240, and she'd trimmed it down to fit in one. Eggs, which had been $1.50 a dozen, jumped past $3.50 after an avian flu outbreak compounded the inflationary pressure. A gallon of milk went from $2.80 to nearly $4.20. Ground beef, once a Tuesday staple, became a once-a-week treat instead of five nights a week, replaced by lentils and canned beans that my father started buying in bulk from a Sam's Club membership he'd resisted getting for years.

We adapted in small, deliberate ways. My mother switched from name-brand cereal to the store's Kroger-brand version, saving about $1.20 per box. My younger brother, twelve at the time, stopped getting the individually wrapped snack packs he liked for school lunches because the per-unit cost had nearly doubled. We started a small vegetable garden that spring — tomatoes, peppers, and zucchini — not as a hobby but as a hedge against produce prices that had risen almost 8% that year alone.

What struck me most wasn't the math itself but how it reshaped our conversations. Dinner planning, once an afterthought, became a Sunday-night budgeting session with a calculator on the kitchen table. My parents started comparing receipts from Kroger, Aldi, and Meijer, driving the extra fifteen minutes to Aldi because the savings, even $15 a week, mattered.

By early 2023, prices had stopped climbing as fast, but they never fell back. The $240 grocery bill became our new normal, not because we'd gotten used to it, but because there was nothing to return to. That year taught me that inflation isn't just a statistic — it's the quiet erosion of choices a family used to take for granted.
