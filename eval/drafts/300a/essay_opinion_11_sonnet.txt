There's a farmers market that sets up every Saturday on the north end of Marion Park in Cleveland, twelve tents, a guy who sells honey from hives he keeps in Bedford Heights, a woman with heirloom tomatoes that cost more than a steak. I love it. I also think it's close to beside the point.

Six blocks south of that park, in the neighborhood we've decided to call Central, there hasn't been a full-service grocery store since a Dave's closed in 2013. What exists instead are two dollar stores, a Marathon station, and a market that mostly sells cigarettes and lottery tickets behind bulletproof glass. If you want a head of broccoli that hasn't been sitting under fluorescent light since March, you need a car, ninety minutes round trip on the 6 bus, or a willingness to pay $4.50 for a bag of shriveled carrots at the corner store. About 12,000 people live in that gap, per the last USDA food access mapping. Most of them don't own cars.

Farmers markets get held up as the fix for this, and I understand the appeal — they're visible, photogenic, easy for a city council member to stand in front of for a ribbon cutting. But a market that runs four hours a week, seasonally, cash-optional, with produce priced for people who already eat well, is not infrastructure. It's a supplement. You cannot build a diet around a stall that's open Saturdays 9 to 1 from May to October and accepts SNAP only if someone remembered to bring the token machine.

What actually closes a food desert is boring: a grocery store with a butcher counter and a pharmacy, open every day, with parking or a bus stop right outside, stocked with $2 rice and $3 dozen eggs, not $9 microgreens. Rebuilding that takes real money — tax abatements, city-backed loans, sometimes a grocer being paid to eat the risk, the way Pittsburgh did with the Hill District Shop 'n Save in 2013 with $10.8 million in public financing. It's slower and less telegenic than a farmers market ribbon cutting. It's also the thing that actually feeds a neighborhood on a Tuesday in February, when there is no market, no tent, no honey guy — just whatever's on the shelf within walking distance.
