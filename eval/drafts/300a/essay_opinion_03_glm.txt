I found the letter last March, wedged inside a copy of Joan Didion's *The White Album* that my mother sent me in 2019. It was three pages, handwritten, dated October 14 of that year, and it described — in her small, forward-leaning cursive — the neighbor's dog, the price of tomatoes at the Saturday market in Davis, California, and her worry that I wasn't sleeping enough in my Brooklyn apartment. I had skimmed it once, apparently, and texted her a thumbs-up.

That thumbs-up has haunted me since.

I'm not a nostalgist. I love iMessage. I sent forty-seven texts yesterday, most of them jokes, most of them good. The speed of digital communication is a genuine miracle, and I would not trade it. But I've come to believe that the handwritten letter occupies territory nothing else can reach, and we've quietly ceded it without noticing.

Here's the thing about a letter: it costs the sender something. My mother spent maybe forty minutes on those three pages. Time is the one gift that can't be refunded or regifted, and handwriting makes the expenditure visible. Every slightly cramped line near the margin says *I kept going anyway*. A text says "I thought of you for four seconds," which is sometimes enough. It is not always enough.

There's also the physical fact of the thing. That letter traveled in an envelope, through sorting facilities, across the country, into a blue mailbox on Fifth Avenue, up to my apartment. My mother's actual hand held the actual paper. When she died last June, I reread it and could almost feel the pressure of the pen. Show me the emoji that carries that.

So I started writing back, before it was too late for her, and I've kept it up since — to my friend Dana in Portland, to my old professor in Chicago, one page a month, no more, which is the point. The constraint is the content. You can't write a paragraph about the neighbor's dog by hand without it meaning something, because your wrist will tell you when you've said enough, and your heart, forced to slow to the pace of the pen, says things the thumb never would.

We don't lack words. We lack slowness. The letter is just slowness you can hold.
