This study examines the effect of British Columbia’s carbon tax on greenhouse-gas emissions from manufacturing plants, focusing on the 2019 policy environment. Although British Columbia introduced its revenue-neutral carbon tax in 2008, the provincial rate increased to C$40 per tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent in April 2019, providing a substantial price signal for fossil-fuel use. Using confidential plant-level data on fuel expenditures, emissions, output, and employment, we construct a manufacturing emissions panel and estimate an augmented difference-in-differences model that exploits variation in plants’ exposure to the tax. The analysis compares more carbon-intensive facilities with less exposed plants while controlling for plant and year effects, sectoral trends, and broader economic conditions.

The results indicate that the carbon tax reduced manufacturing-plant greenhouse-gas emissions by approximately 4% relative to the counterfactual trajectory. The decline was driven primarily by a reduction in emissions intensity, which fell by about 6%, while plant output increased by approximately 1.8%. These findings suggest that the policy did not reduce emissions solely through contraction in manufacturing activity or plant closures. Instead, firms appear to have responded by producing more output with less fossil-fuel energy, consistent with investment in energy-efficiency and productivity-enhancing technologies. The revenue-neutral design, which recycled tax revenues through reductions in corporate income taxes and other measures, may have supported this adjustment by lowering the cost of abatement investments.

Overall, the evidence suggests that British Columbia’s carbon-pricing regime generated measurable emissions reductions in manufacturing without suppressing aggregate plant output. However, the estimates should not be interpreted as identifying the independent effect of a new 2019 tax, because the policy was introduced in 2008 and its 2019 rate represented an escalation of an existing program.
