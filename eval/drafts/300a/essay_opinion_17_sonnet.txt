I go to the Enoch Pratt Free Library on Cathedral Street in Baltimore most Tuesdays, and I have never once been asked to buy anything to stay.

That sentence shouldn't sound remarkable. But sit in a public place in America for three hours — a coffee shop, a mall food court, a hotel lobby — and see how long before someone asks what you're purchasing. The library is one of the last rooms in the country where the price of admission is zero and the terms of service are simply: be a person. Enoch Pratt doesn't ask your zip code. The Los Angeles Public Library's 73 branches don't check your immigration status. The Brooklyn Public Library handed out 6,600 free digital library cards to teenagers nationwide in 2022 after a wave of book bans, so kids in Texas and Florida could read what their own libraries had pulled from the shelves.

I think about what else is left. The park bench, maybe, until a city installs the armrests that make lying down impossible — there are more than 500 documented cases of hostile architecture in major U.S. cities since 2014. The church, if you belong to one. The DMV, which is public but not exactly a place anyone lingers. Everything else has been quietly re-metered: the mall became a place to be watched by security guards paid by the property owner, the sidewalk café requires a $7 espresso as cover charge, even the internet — once the great leveler — now runs on data brokers who sell your library-adjacent Google searches to advertisers. Libraries, by contrast, run on the Library Bill of Rights, first adopted by the American Library Association in 1939, which still holds that materials shouldn't be excluded because of the origin or views of those contributing to their creation.

None of this is an accident. Andrew Carnegie funded 1,689 library buildings in the U.S. between 1883 and 1929 on the condition that towns pledge public tax money to keep them free forever — he understood, better than most robber barons, that democracy needs rooms that don't ask you to pay rent on your own citizenship.

Sixty-one percent of Americans visited a library or library website in the past year, per Pew's 2024 survey — more than voted in the last midterm. That's not nostalgia. That's the last commons still standing, and we should notice before it isn't.
