Between 1849 and roughly 1860, Harriet Tubman made about thirteen return trips into the slaveholding South, and each one was a direct violation of federal law. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 made it a crime not just to escape bondage but to help anyone else do so: it imposed fines up to $1,000 and jail time on abettors, denied accused fugitives a jury trial, and compelled ordinary citizens across the North to assist in captures on demand. Tubman, who had fled slavery in Dorchester County, Maryland, in 1849, answered that law by going back into exactly the territory it was designed to police.

Her method turned the Act's own machinery against itself. She traveled at night, often in winter when patrols were thinner and darkness lasted longer, and used the North Star and word-of-mouth contacts along what became known as the Underground Railroad to move people north. After 1850, the law's reach into free states meant Philadelphia and even New York were no longer safe endpoints, so Tubman extended her routes to St. Catharines, Ontario, in Canada, where British law placed escapees beyond the reach of U.S. marshals. She is credited with personally leading around seventy enslaved people to freedom, including members of her own family, and she did it while a bounty reportedly reaching $40,000 was rumored to hang over her capture.

What made her missions a direct defiance of the Act, rather than simple escape, is that she kept returning. The law assumed fear would seal the border in one direction; Tubman crossed it repeatedly, in the direction of greatest danger, to bring others out. She carried a pistol, both for self-protection and, according to later accounts, to press forward anyone who lost nerve and threatened to turn back and expose the group. During the Civil War she extended this defiance further, serving as a scout and spy for the Union Army and helping lead the 1863 Combahee River raid in South Carolina, which freed over 700 enslaved people in a single night.

Tubman's thirteen missions show that the Fugitive Slave Act, however severe on paper, could not survive contact with someone willing to treat its penalties as a cost of doing the work rather than a deterrent from it.
